Meta Releases First Frontier AI Models: Muse Wonder and Muse Wonder Full

Key Highlights
  • Meta's Superintelligence Lab launched Muse Wonder and Muse Wonder Full, its first frontier-class models targeting professional agentic tasks in law, finance, and healthcare.
  • The models feature 'active memory' that retains user preferences across sessions, with performance benchmarks matching leading rivals.
  • Available now via Meta Model API at $4/$20 per million tokens; Muse Wonder Full is exclusive to Muse Code subscribers.
Industry Insight

Meta entering frontier AI reshapes its ad-product roadmap; agencies should track how Muse Wonder capabilities feed into Advantage+ and AI-driven campaign automation.

Pattern Watch

Meta's frontier model entry signals its ad systems will increasingly run on proprietary AI — agencies should track how Muse Wonder capabilities integrate into Advantage+ creative automation and Reels ad generation over the next two quarters.

Read our full write-up

Meta's Superintelligence Lab has released Muse Wonder and Muse Wonder Full, marking the company's formal entry into the frontier model tier. Unlike the lightweight, low-latency Muse Spark, the Wonder series is built for complex, multi-step agentic workflows and advanced coding tasks. On standard benchmarks, Wonder scores are comparable to Fable 5.1 and GPT-6 Astra, confirming Meta has moved beyond competing solely in the efficiency model segment.

The two models carry distinct positioning. Muse Wonder is available via the Meta Model API at $4 per million input tokens and $20 per million output tokens, pricing that aligns with GPT-6 Sol and Opus 5.5 and targets enterprise developers on a cost-performance basis. Muse Wonder Full is a higher-capability variant exclusive to Muse Code subscribers, with no external API access, forming a closed premium tier. Both models serve as the core engines powering the Muse agent, signaling that Meta's commercial intent is to use model capability to anchor a broader agentic application ecosystem.

The standout technical feature is Active Memory. Research scientist Kavya Kopparapu described the design goal as ensuring the model can retain and synthesize what matters to a user across days, weeks, and months of sessions and tasks. Wonder's lead over Fable, Opus, and Astra on MemoryBench—a continual learning and memory recall benchmark—substantiates this claim beyond marketing language. For enterprise deployments, persistent memory means agents can maintain consistent personalized context across work sessions, significantly reducing the overhead of repeated prompting.

The models were trained at Meta's Prometheus data center in central Ohio, which opened in 2026, reflecting the returns now materializing from Meta's sustained infrastructure investment. Organizationally, this release is the first frontier output since Scale AI founder Alexandr Wang restructured Meta AI in August 2025. Meta has described the Wonder series as the next step on its scaling ladder and a major leap toward personal superintelligence—a strategic narrative that is now backed by measurable product milestones rather than forward projections alone.

Zhipu GLM-5.3-Highspeed Hits 490 Tokens/s with 1M Context Window

Key Highlights
  • GLM-5.3-Highspeed reached 490 tokens/s in tests — 2.4× faster than last month's FlashX — generating 10,000 tokens in approximately 20 seconds.
  • Supports a 1M token context window with up to 131,072 tokens output, plus three reasoning intensity tiers: low, high, and max.
  • Currently rolling out to select Zcode 3.15.1 subscribers; large-scale commercial pricing has not yet been announced.
Industry Insight

GLM-5.3's speed leap cuts the cost of bulk Chinese content generation, directly benefiting GBA agencies running high-volume copywriting or social content workflows.

Read our full write-up

GLM-5.3-Highspeed from Zhipu AI has recorded a generation speed of 490 tokens per second in the latest user tests, more than double the throughput of the GLM-5.3-FlashX variant at approximately 200 TPS. In practical terms, generating 10,000 tokens takes roughly 20 seconds at this rate—a meaningful threshold for latency-sensitive applications such as real-time code completion, long-form drafting, and multi-turn dialogue. Placing this in the context of the broader GLM-5 family—standard Flash on the Z.ai API runs at around 60 TPS, and the previous-generation GLM-5.1-Highspeed from May 2026 reached approximately 400 TPS—the efficiency gains are non-linear and represent a step change rather than incremental tuning.

On the specification side, GLM-5.3-Highspeed retains the full capability architecture of the flagship version. It supports a one-million-token context window, a maximum output length of 131,072 tokens (approximately 128k), and three selectable reasoning intensities—low, high, and max—allowing users to balance speed against depth depending on task complexity. The million-token context enables workflows involving large document analysis and cross-file code review, placing it among the widest context windows currently available in production models.

The rollout follows a tiered approach: GLM-5.3-Highspeed is currently being pushed selectively to Zcode 3.15.1 subscribers on a randomized basis, with Lite-tier users not yet broadly covered. Early users describe the experience as fast output paired with noticeably longer reasoning time and solid accuracy. Unconfirmed speculation in developer communities links the model to Omen Alpha, an anonymous model added to OpenCode in September 2026, though Zhipu has not officially commented on any connection.

At the industry level, inference speed is increasingly a primary competitive dimension rather than a secondary metric. For developer tooling, real-time translation, and high-volume ad copy generation, 490 TPS establishes a practical deployment baseline. Zhipu's focus on engineering throughput offers a differentiated position relative to Meta and Anthropic, which are emphasizing agentic capability and long-term memory, reflecting how leading model vendors are now competing on operational efficiency to capture production deployments beyond the frontier benchmark leaderboards.

Microsoft President Brad Smith Says UK Is Best Placed to Lead Global AI Regulation

Since Yesterday

On October 11 we covered Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella's call for an AI emergency brake. Today Microsoft President Brad Smith maps a concrete regulatory path: naming the UK's AISI as the best candidate to lead globally, and calling for mandatory breach-notification laws and pre-release third-party audits — from Nadella's 'we need brakes' to Smith's 'here's who should build them.'

Key Highlights
  • Microsoft President Brad Smith urged urgent AI legislation, naming the UK's AISI as the best-positioned global body — free from EU political entanglements — to lead.
  • Smith proposed three concrete policies: mandatory AI breach-notification laws, pre-release third-party audits, and required AI kill-switch capabilities.
  • The UK AI Security Institute confirmed it retains authority to test frontier models, unaffected by recent US political pressure on its independence.
Industry Insight

A binding AI regulatory framework will shape frontier model deployment timelines and adtech compliance costs; agencies should track UK AISI developments as a leading indicator for Asia-Pacific rules.

Read our full write-up

Microsoft President Brad Smith has publicly called for binding AI regulation and named the United Kingdom as the country best positioned to lead the process globally. His central argument rests on a pointed comparison: everyday consumer goods such as milk and household appliances face more rigorous regulatory scrutiny than AI systems with comparable or greater societal impact, an asymmetry that reflects a structural lag between policy frameworks and technological reality.

Smith highlighted the UK's AI Security Institute, established in 2023, as the institutional foundation for this leadership role. AISI already holds pre-deployment access to frontier AI models from leading developers—an arrangement that is genuinely novel among major technology markets. Smith's case for the UK over the US or EU turns on political neutrality: as he put it, in Washington people do not view the UK the way they view Brussels, and that difference is a strategic asset. The EU's AI Act carries the weight of member-state political negotiations, while the US has not achieved unified federal AI legislation. The UK's position allows it to serve as a credible intermediary between the two blocs.

Smith reserved clear skepticism for voluntary self-regulation, describing the White House tech summit document as a good step while immediately noting its structural limitation: only responsible actors comply with voluntary commitments, and the companies that pose the greatest risk are precisely those least likely to follow through. This logic underpins his three specific policy proposals: a legal obligation for companies to report breaches caused by rogue AI agents; mandatory approval from an independent outside evaluator before any AI product is released; and the establishment of kill switches that can halt AI systems when human oversight is materially lost.

The timing of Smith's remarks carries its own significance. On the same day, October 12, 2026, reports emerged that the Trump administration had asked OpenAI and Anthropic to withhold model access from UK testers, a claim AISI confirmed was not reflected in actual practice. That episode underscores the geopolitical dimensions of AI governance that Smith's framework is navigating. By describing AI as simultaneously a weapon and a tool carrying global cybersecurity risks, Smith frames cross-border regulatory coordination not as a jurisdictional question but as a shared security imperative.

Ad Industry Tallies Early Losses from Apple's Safari Tracking Block as eCPMs Drop 10.5%

Since Yesterday

On October 8 we reported iOS 27's ad-tech blacklist, with The Trade Desk and LiveRamp among the first blocked. Today Digiday quantifies the first-week damage: eCPMs down 10.5%, The Trade Desk's ad server fix won't reach users until late October — and only ~8% of users have updated, meaning the full impact is still ahead as Q4 peaks.

Key Highlights
  • Apple's Safari update silently blocked major ad-tracking vendors with no advance notice; early data shows eCPMs down an average of 10.5% among affected users in week one.
  • The Trade Desk's ad server was blocked but temporarily patched; a full fix for iOS Safari won't reach users until late October.
  • Only ~8% of users have updated so far — as the update rate rises heading into Q4, the full scale of impact is still ahead.
Industry Insight

Q4 programmatic revenue is directly hit; agencies relying on cross-site identity tracking should urgently evaluate first-party data alternatives before the full iOS user base updates.

Pattern Watch

Each major iOS update triggers another round of ad-tech attrition. Short-term eCPM drops are now predictable; long-term beneficiaries are brands with first-party data assets and agencies that can guide clients through the walled-garden transition.

Read our full write-up

Apple's latest Safari tracking block differs from its 2017 and 2021 predecessors in one critical operational respect: the blocklist is determined unilaterally and kept confidential, meaning the industry has no visible criteria against which to assess exposure in advance. This information asymmetry makes systematic preparation nearly impossible; affected parties can only audit their own situation after the impact has already materialized.

Data from ad technology platform Aditude provides the clearest early picture of scale. Across publishers using the platform, Safari—covering iPhone and iPad—accounts for 11.7% of ads served and 11.4% of ad revenue. Among users who have already upgraded to the latest iOS version, eCPMs dropped 10.5%. With approximately 8% of users updated as of the reporting date, the realized overall revenue impact is estimated at roughly 1%. That figure carries a compounding warning: as update rates climb through Q4, the most commercially valuable quarter of the advertising calendar, the damage will scale proportionally.

The Trade Desk's ad server situation adds a layer of ambiguity. Engineers discovered that the server had been quietly removed from the blocklist in a test iOS build following the filing of a bug report—yet current production users remain blocked, with a fix not expected until the end of October at the earliest. Index Exchange CEO Andrew Casale posed the question directly: is this a restoration, or an admission of a significant error? Casale also criticized the stark contrast between Apple's permissive approach in the app ecosystem and its behavior on the open web, describing it as quite shocking. The timing, arriving at the start of the most lucrative ad period of the year, compounds the commercial pressure on publishers dependent on programmatic buying.

Industry interpretation of Apple's motivation centers on competitive self-interest. One publisher executive described the move as blatant, arguing that anything making advertising more difficult ultimately benefits Apple's own advertising business—App Store and Apple Search Ads revenue has grown steadily in recent years. IAB Tech Lab's repeated attempts to engage Apple on privacy policy since the late 2010s have produced little measurable result, consistent with Apple's historical pattern of acting unilaterally on ecosystem rules. Consultant Davide Rosamilia's assessment that things will not improve and will only get worse appears to reflect a broad industry consensus. For publishers with significant programmatic exposure on Safari inventory, the current partial-coverage window may represent a final opportunity to reassess revenue diversification before full update rollout arrives.

Apple's iOS 27 Blocks Hundreds More Programmatic Data Companies Beyond Initial Names

Since Yesterday

On October 8 we reported the iOS 27 blacklist targeting The Trade Desk and LiveRamp. Today Campaign Asia reveals the block has expanded dramatically to hundreds of programmatic data companies — far beyond the initial names — putting agencies' entire mobile data pipeline at risk. The situation is significantly worse than first reported.

Key Highlights
  • iOS 27's ad-tech blocks extend far beyond the initially disclosed vendors to hundreds of programmatic data companies across the supply chain.
  • The entire mobile programmatic pipeline is affected — DMPs, DSPs, and data aggregators all impacted — with vendors unable to patch fast enough to keep pace with Apple's restrictions.
  • Industry observers warn this accelerates the long-term structural decline of third-party mobile audience data, making first-party data strategy the only reliable path forward for agencies.
Industry Insight

Wider iOS blocks will strip mobile data access from hundreds of ad-tech vendors; agencies must build first-party data infrastructure proactively rather than waiting for vendor-by-vendor patches.

Read our full write-up

Apple's iOS 27 introduced a dynamic blocking mechanism built on WebKit's "ContentRuleList" — a list stored in a private GitHub repository that Apple can update at any time without pushing a software release. The list operates through two new filter conditions: `isRequestToKnownCrossSiteTracker` and `!supportsFingerprintingScriptRequests`, the first intercepting requests from known cross-site trackers and the second excluding scripts deemed to support fingerprinting. Because iOS mandates WebKit for all browsers, the block extends to every browser on iPhone and iPad — not just Safari — a reach far exceeding what the industry initially anticipated.

When the story first broke, the named companies were heavyweights in programmatic advertising: The Trade Desk (and its UID 2.0 identity solution), LiveRamp, ID5, Permutive, and Experian's Audigent. The Trade Desk was subsequently granted a partial reprieve — its ad server domain was briefly removed from the list — leading some to speculate Apple might negotiate individually. However, subsequent AdExchanger reporting revealed the list had expanded from a handful of names to hundreds of CDPs, ad tech vendors, martech platforms, data sellers, and identity graph operators.

What unsettles the industry most about this list is its opacity: vendors may be added without any notification, only discovering their blocked status when traffic or match rates drop unexpectedly. IAB Tech Lab's Rowena Lam identified the dynamic-update capability as the core threat, since it means no vendor can rely on a stable status. Investment bankers noted they would not currently back a fundraise for an identity company. By contrast, Zeta's LiveIntent, Publicis-owned Lotame, and Yahoo's ConnectID have not appeared on the list — though the industry widely expects the list to keep evolving.

For agencies, the impact is not an abstract technical matter. It severs the mobile audience data pipeline for programmatic campaigns that rely on these vendors for audience identification, frequency capping, and cross-device attribution. Publishers who depend on Safari and iPhone users for premium inventory are equally exposed, as third-party IDs fail across the iOS environment. Most discussions at Advertising Week New York were held off the record, with executives accelerating evaluation of first-party data strategies and server-side solutions as alternative paths forward.

Honeyjar Launches AI PR Platform, Attracts 10,000 Waitlist Signups in 24 Hours

Key Highlights

  • The platform attracted over 10,000 waitlist signups within 24 hours of launch, with beta testers including Nextdoor and various startups and nonprofits.
  • The tool challenges traditional wire services like PR Newswire by replacing mass-broadcast distribution with AI-personalized journalist matching.
Industry Insight

Agencies can evaluate Honeyjar to streamline client PR workflows — from press release drafting to journalist matching — and reduce time spent on routine media outreach tasks.

Read our full write-up

Honeyjar was co-founded by two practitioners with deep public relations and communications backgrounds: CEO Michelle Masek, former SVP of marketing and communications at Obvious Ventures, and CCO Nadia Jamshidi, who brings decades of agency and in-house PR experience. The founders built the platform around a persistently underserved pain point: companies contain large numbers of what Masek calls "unassigned communicators" — founders, marketers, investors, authors, and creators — who carry real communications responsibilities without professional PR support, relying on personal judgment to navigate outreach. Honeyjar's design goal is to encode senior-level PR judgment into AI workflows and close that gap.

The platform's core functionality spans four areas: automated drafting of press releases and announcements; journalist matching that cross-references a media database to identify the most relevant contacts for a given pitch; coverage tracking; and proactive identification of speaking and interview opportunities aligned with the client's expertise. Unlike some generative tools, Honeyjar explicitly keeps humans in the loop — the AI drafts, but the user decides whether to send or publish. Masek describes the platform's operating principle as integrating "signals, context, strategy, judgment, timing, and execution," emphasizing that PR effectiveness depends not just on copy generation but on systematic prioritization of what to say and when.

Within 24 hours of opening its waitlist, Honeyjar accumulated more than 10,000 signups, signaling strong latent demand for automated communications tools. Early beta participants include Nextdoor, robotics startup Coco Robotics, Bloomberg Beta, and PR agencies Mission North and Full Picture — spanning social platforms, deep tech, venture capital, and traditional PR services. The diversity of early testers suggests the founders are deliberately stress-testing whether Honeyjar delivers consistent value across organizations of very different scales.

Honeyjar chose TechCrunch Disrupt 2026 Startup Battlefield 200 as its public debut stage — a choice that is itself a communications strategy, surfacing the platform in front of the densest concentration of technology media at once. Funding amounts and pricing have not been disclosed, indicating the platform remains in a demand-signal phase. For the marketing and PR industry, the most significant question Honeyjar raises is whether it can genuinely encode strategic judgment — not just writing productivity — into an AI system. If it delivers on that promise, it poses a structural challenge to the traditional value proposition of communications agencies.

Ambi Raises $26M for Ambient AI Assistant That Listens in the Background

Key Highlights
  • Ambi raised $26M led by Luminous Ventures, offering continuous ambient recording across iPhone, Mac, and Apple Watch.
  • The app proactively surfaces context from calendars and knowledge bases during meetings; ambient recording is off by default to protect privacy.
  • Founded by a former Plaud executive, with teams across San Francisco and China, and a dedicated hardware device with screen and camera planned.
Industry Insight

Ambient AI recording tools offer potential for agency meeting workflows, but raise data consent and privacy compliance questions that must be assessed before deployment in client-facing contexts.

Read our full write-up

Ambi's funding round was led by Luminous Ventures — backers of Meituan and Unitree Robotics — with ODM manufacturer Huaqin co-leading. Huaqin's participation signals not just capital but potential manufacturing capacity for the company's planned standalone hardware. Ambi currently employs more than 50 people across San Francisco, Beijing, Shanghai, and Shenzhen, and is actively hiring in go-to-market and AI speech recognition engineering. Founder and CEO Peter Mo previously worked at Plaud, an AI meeting-recorder hardware company, and his experience navigating the tension between ambient audio processing and user privacy directly shapes Ambi's product philosophy.

Ambi's core design principle is persistent contextual awareness: with user permission, the app continuously captures ambient audio and syncs context seamlessly across iPhone, Mac, and Apple Watch. During meetings, the system draws on the user's calendar, a self-built knowledge base, and live web data to surface relevant suggestions proactively — without requiring the user to issue a query. The forthcoming desktop app expands this further: in addition to microphone input, it will monitor screen activity and convert daily work into a personalized knowledge base that AI suggestions can draw on, making recommendations more tailored the more it is used.

Ambient listening is disabled by default; users must actively enable it, and when disabled the app functions as a conventional AI assistant. The company states that recordings are not stored on its servers and that data is fully encrypted. Mo acknowledged directly: "We, as an industry, haven't solved the problem of disclosure" — how to ensure people in the room know they are being recorded. That candor drew mixed reactions: some read it as responsible transparency, others as evidence that regulatory frameworks have not kept pace with product design. The planned standalone hardware device, equipped with a screen, speaker, camera, and microphone array, is designed for office and meeting-room contexts to deliver ambient awareness with less interruption than a mobile phone.

For the marketing and advertising industry, Ambi represents an early form of a new data infrastructure category: AI systems shifting from passive query-responders to active context monitors that continuously accumulate fine-grained signals about user preferences, decision contexts, and work rhythms. If this technology achieves enterprise adoption, the personalized knowledge graphs it generates could become a new dimension for ad targeting and content personalization — while also suggesting that the current tracking architecture centered on cookies and device IDs faces a deeper structural replacement from below.

HK Agencies Must Reinvest AI Cost Savings into Fresh IP, Not Just Capture Efficiencies, Says IAB

Key Highlights
  • IAB Hong Kong Vice Chair Arthur Chan noted AI has dramatically lowered the cost threshold for cross-market campaign testing, making multi-market experimentation accessible to mid-size brands.
  • Chan warned agencies against positioning 'AI saves money' as their only selling point — savings should be reinvested in original IP and immersive experiences that are harder to replicate.
  • The argument reflects industry-wide concern about AI commoditization: when every agency can cut costs with AI, strategic creative capability becomes the real competitive moat.
Industry Insight

Directly addresses HK ad agency AI strategy, offering a differentiated growth narrative beyond cost-cutting that agencies can use to reframe their AI value proposition to clients.

Read our full write-up

Arthur Chan's argument rests on a structural observation: AI has reduced the marginal cost of testing creative across markets. Previously, running five creative directions simultaneously across three Southeast Asian markets carried a production cost that posed a real threshold. AI generation tools have compressed timelines and cut headcount requirements, making multi-market, small-batch, rapid-iteration testing financially viable for the first time. This shift in the cost center is the premise of Chan's argument — he does not dispute the competitive pressure of lower fees but contends that the premise itself opens a door that was previously closed.

Chan argues that if agencies convert AI efficiency gains solely into lower quotes for clients, they are choosing a path of accelerating commoditization. Competitors have access to the same tools, and a price war ends in industry-wide margin compression. His alternative is to reinvest the savings — time, personnel, and budget — into original IP and experience design, cultivating new revenue forms that audiences or brands will pay a premium for. The practical implication is a business model restructuring: shifting from billing for execution hours to capturing higher unit value through licensing, IP partnerships, or branded experience consulting.

The structural conditions of the Hong Kong agency market add particular urgency to this argument. With a limited local market and client budgets generally lower than regional peers, agencies competing primarily on execution efficiency have little room to maneuver. As AI tools lower the technical barriers to execution, Hong Kong agencies face diminishing prospects for sustainable differentiation at the production layer. Chan's framing implies a judgment about local positioning: the durable competitive advantage of Hong Kong agencies more likely lies in cultural insight, accumulated creative IP, and relationship depth than in speed or price.

Chan's role as IAB Hong Kong vice chair gives this position a degree of institutional weight. The IAB has traditionally emphasized technology adoption and market standards; for Chan to foreground original IP over tool efficiency at this juncture reads as a deliberate public recalibration — asserting, at the moment AI tools are becoming ubiquitous and agencies face collective pressure to cut fees, that human creative judgment remains the non-outsourceable core asset.
