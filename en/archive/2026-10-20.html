Anthropic Data: AI Agents Log 6× More Hours Than Humans, Junior Roles Drop to 14%

Key Highlights
  • AI agents log 6× more hours than human employees at Anthropic, reaching 19:1 in the research division.
  • Junior technical staff share fell from 21% to 14%; new junior hires dropped from 27% to 9%.
  • Engineers ship 5.1× more code commits per person, reflecting dramatically higher individual output with AI assistance.
Industry Insight

AI agents are displacing junior roles — agencies should assess equivalent workforce pressure now.

Read our full write-up

Anthropic's recently published internal workforce analysis reveals a structural shift in how the company is organized. The most striking headline figure: junior technical staff have fallen from 21% of the technical workforce in early 2025 to just 14% today. New junior technical hires show an even sharper decline — dropping from 27% of all new hires to just 9%. Technical staff as a share of total headcount also contracted, from 61% to 56%, driven by growth in non-technical business functions and by non-engineers increasingly using AI tools to complete tasks that once required developer support.

The productivity data accompanying these figures deserves close attention from agency leaders in Hong Kong. AI agents — specifically Claude — now log working hours at a ratio of 19:1 versus human researchers in Anthropic's research division. Engineers are producing five times more code commits without any measurable decline in quality. An April 2026 internal survey found 27% of employees reported significant productivity gains attributable to Claude, while 20% described their role as having shifted from direct execution to 'managing multiple Claude instances.' This redefines what a high-value employee looks like: the premium is increasingly on people who can frame tasks, audit AI output, and bear accountability — not those who simply execute.

Counter-intuitively, these efficiency gains have not reduced meeting time. Per-employee meeting time rose 20% year-over-year overall, and 16% among technical contributors specifically. Anthropic attributes this to a shift in the nature of human work: once AI handles the production volume, human attention becomes the bottleneck for trust-building and accountability verification, making in-person coordination more critical rather than less. One engineer described the experience as 'babysitting' — simultaneously managing multiple Claude instances, each demanding meaningful attention.

For Hong Kong marketing agencies evaluating their own AI transition roadmap, this report offers a rare early-stage data point from an organization that is arguably the world's most AI-saturated workforce. Three practical implications stand out. First, hiring strategy needs recalibration: junior execution roles will not vanish entirely, but compression is near-certain, and any new junior hire must be prepared from day one to manage AI output rather than replace it. Second, management structures must budget for more lateral coordination overhead — as AI penetration increases, cross-functional decision alignment and accountability definition consistently cost more than anticipated. Third, Anthropic explicitly positions itself as an early indicator for economy-wide labor shifts; agencies that begin tracking analogous internal metrics now will be far better positioned to plan workforce strategy 12 to 24 months ahead.

Industry Insight

Model loyalty at leading AI platforms is fluid — agencies should reassess toolchain lock-in risk.

Read our full write-up

At TechCrunch Disrupt in October 2026, Replit CEO Amjad Masad publicly announced that the platform had switched its default AI model from Anthropic's Claude to OpenAI, naming OpenAI the 'preferred partner' powering Replit Agent. Replit had been among Anthropic's top-five paying customers — a relationship that helped Replit grow its revenue roughly tenfold. This switch marks one of the most significant customer departures in the AI foundation model market to date.

Masad cited three reasons: performance (internal benchmarks showed OpenAI models outperform Claude when building vibe-coded applications from scratch), cost (more competitive pricing), and partnership quality (OpenAI described as 'a better partner for now'). The core lesson of this episode is that AI model vendor stickiness is far lower than traditional software lock-in. The 'default' slot is the true strategic asset: the vast majority of users never manually switch models, so losing default status means losing the bulk of platform traffic. For Hong Kong agencies building or procuring AI-powered tools, two practical points stand out. First, when evaluating any AI platform, actively ask what the underlying model is and what the vendor's policy is on switching — a workflow designed around a specific model's capabilities may degrade silently after a substitution. Second, over-reliance on a single model vendor creates architectural fragility; maintaining substitutability at the model layer is a basic risk-management principle. Replit's enterprise revenue growing from approximately 2% in 2025 to 15% in 2026 — with projections exceeding 50% by 2027 — also confirms that enterprise clients are placing increasing weight on AI tooling vendor stability.

Industry Insight

AI agent purchasing is scaling fast — agencies with retail or travel clients must rethink attribution models now.

Pattern Watch

Autonomous AI agent purchasing is forming a new buying channel. Over the next twelve months, expect direct procurement links between brand AI agents (like Meta Muse) and retailer inventory systems — advertising's role will shift from influencing human decisions to influencing the behavior settings of agents.

Read our full write-up

Stripe has disclosed that AI agent transaction counts on its platform surged 38-fold month-over-month in September, with payment volume climbing 20-fold over the same period. While Stripe declined to release absolute figures, these multipliers carry significant signal value — they represent agentic commerce crossing from theoretical possibility into quantifiable commercial reality. Approximately 200 AI agents are now active on Stripe, including notable examples such as Meta's Muse and Grok Bot. Anthropic and OpenAI have integrated with Stripe's Link platform, though not yet for agent purchasing use cases.

By industry, retail accounts for 36% of agent transaction counts and travel represents 28% of payment volume. The typical agent transaction runs around $100, though high-value scenarios — such as flight bookings and premium furniture purchases — can reach several thousand dollars. Stripe's technical architecture issues a proxy 16-digit card number rather than exposing users' actual payment credentials, with user-configurable spending limits designed to balance automation efficiency with financial control. Stripe executive Dan Hill acknowledged the space remains 'in its very early days,' with PayPal and Shopify's Shop Pay already moving in the same direction.

For Hong Kong agencies serving retail and travel clients, this data points toward a structural disruption that will reshape the foundations of conversion attribution. Traditional attribution logic assumes a human decision-maker: a human clicks an ad, browses a product page, and completes checkout. Once an AI agent becomes the transacting party, the fundamental premise of that attribution chain breaks down. Agent purchasing behavior may not originate from ad exposure at all — it may stem from the agent's training data, the user's historical preferences, or real-time price comparison logic. A brand's prominence in 'agent decision space' is beginning to emerge as a distinct competitive dimension in marketing.

Three areas require forward planning for agencies. First, conversion funnel measurement must evolve: click-through rates and page view metrics premised on human behavior offer limited insight into agent-initiated transactions, and agencies should work with clients to define how agentic purchases will be tracked and attributed. Second, the structured quality of a retail or travel client's product data will directly influence how readily AI agents select their offerings — clean, complete, machine-readable product information is emerging as a new form of search optimization. Third, absolute transaction volumes remain small and the month-over-month multipliers largely reflect a low base effect; agencies should place this on a 12-to-18-month strategic watch list rather than treating it as requiring immediate comprehensive response.

Two German Courts Rule Google Is Liable as Publisher for AI Overview Content

Key Highlights
  • Both German courts found Google AI Overviews represent Google's own assertions, establishing direct publisher liability for AI-generated search summaries.
  • The Aachen injunction sets a €250,000 fine per violation, formally establishing an accountability threshold for AI search.
  • The ruling may prompt similar actions across Europe, influencing how Google designs its AI search products globally.
Industry Insight

Europe is building a publisher-liability model for AI search — brand monitoring and correction protocols are now essential.

Pattern Watch

European courts are establishing publisher liability for AI search summaries. If confirmed at EU level, Google may limit specific brand and product claims in AI Overviews — accelerating GEO from a nice-to-have to a baseline requirement.

Read our full write-up

In 2026, two German regional courts issued rulings on Google AI Overviews that establish a legal principle with no clear precedent elsewhere in the world: AI-generated search summaries constitute the platform's own original statements rather than neutral references to third-party information, making Google liable as a publisher.

The two cases arose from very different facts but reached strikingly consistent conclusions. Munich Regional Court I ruled in May 2026 that Google's AI had falsely linked two Munich publishers to fraud schemes, classifying the AI Overviews as Google's own statements rather than search result links. Google appealed and ultimately settled out of court, but the underlying legal reasoning was never overturned. The Aachen case was more dramatic: a neuroradiology professor discovered that Google's AI Mode had attributed a sexual assault allegation involving a different professor to him, complete with fabricated multi-paragraph accounts and invented legal proceedings. After Google failed to act for more than two weeks following notification, the Aachen court issued an emergency injunction without hearing Google's response, with potential fines of up to €250,000 per violation.

The Aachen court explicitly cited Munich's reasoning, finding that Google's AI produces 'independent, new, and substantive statements' rather than summaries of external sources. The court further noted that Google possesses the technical capability to verify its AI outputs against source websites and chose not to do so — a finding that reinforces the rationale for treating Google as an active publisher rather than a passive intermediary.

For Hong Kong marketing agencies, these rulings carry direct practical implications for brand monitoring services. European court decisions do not bind Hong Kong directly, but multinational clients — particularly brands with European operations — will need to incorporate AI search monitoring into their global brand protection strategy. The more fundamental issue is that AI Overviews now occupy the most prominent position on many search result pages; inaccurate statements about a brand appearing there can spread at a speed and scale that far exceeds traditional negative press. Agencies should proactively advise clients to include Google AI Overviews, Microsoft Copilot search summaries, and equivalent AI-generated responses within their existing brand monitoring frameworks — regularly testing brand keywords across major AI search tools and establishing correction and escalation procedures. The 'publisher liability' theory established by the German courts also signals that brand owners will have increasingly clear legal recourse when AI search summaries produce false statements about their organizations.

Meta Revised Terms Still Allow Business Website Data Collection — Review Before Oct 30

Key Highlights
  • Meta initially added language granting broad crawling rights over business website domains, pulled after legal experts flagged it as overreach.
  • Revised terms still authorize using business website data for ad systems; companies can signal preferences via robots.txt but cannot fully opt out.
  • Complete opt-out requires deleting the Meta account — agencies must clearly explain this constraint and its data implications to clients.
Industry Insight

Agencies managing client Facebook Business accounts must review the revised terms before Oct 30 and brief clients on data implications.

Read our full write-up

In early October 2026, Meta released updated terms of service that immediately drew scrutiny from the legal community. The original draft explicitly authorized Meta to use 'automated software (such as web scrapers, bots, robots, spiders, crawlers)' to access all domains and URLs linked to business accounts, with the ability to periodically pull updates for training and research purposes. Legal experts cited by Nieman Lab noted the original language amounted to a near-unlimited grant of commercial data collection rights, with account deletion before October 30 as the only available opt-out.

Under public pressure, Meta spokesperson Daniel Roberts clarified that the crawling provisions 'only applied to our ads and business products,' with revised language reflecting that narrowing. However, a careful reading of the updated terms confirms Meta still retains the right to collect business website data for ad-targeting optimization. In practical terms, any business continuing to use Meta's advertising system implicitly consents to having its website content systematically harvested to improve ad delivery — a significant data-strategy implication in the current era of AI-driven ad optimization.

On the opt-out mechanism, Meta confirmed that businesses may use robots.txt to 'signal their preferences.' Critically, robots.txt is a voluntary protocol with no binding legal force, and Meta's own wording stops short of any compliance guarantee. This means robots.txt provides no hard legal protection — it is a statement of preference, not a contractual right. Full opt-out remains only possible by deleting the account entirely, which effectively means abandoning access to Meta's entire advertising ecosystem.

For Hong Kong agencies, the most urgent action is briefing clients with Meta Business accounts before the October 30 deadline. Key questions to resolve include: whether the client's website has robots.txt configured, whether its current coverage is sufficient, and whether the client objects to Meta using their site data for AI model training or ad optimization. Clients in regulated industries — financial services, healthcare — face particular exposure, as their website data may contain sensitive or classified information. Agencies should also review client service agreements to ensure adequate disclosure of data collection practices, reducing future legal liability.

Meta has faced multiple lawsuits over scraping practices in recent years. While this revision appears to narrow the original terms, it simultaneously codifies the legal basis for ad-related data collection in clearer language than before. Agencies should treat this as a chapter in the broader data sovereignty story: businesses advertising on Meta are not merely buying reach — they are granting a standing, renewable license for their digital assets to be systematically collected and used.

Industry Insight

Agencies producing AI ad creatives must prepare for stricter labeling rules to avoid content removal.

Read our full write-up

Instagram head Adam Mosseri recently made a public statement expressing serious concern about the 'AI slop' phenomenon. The immediate trigger was an artist who publicly complained that AI tools were being used to massively replicate their personal artistic style across the platform, with existing mechanisms failing to provide any meaningful protection. Mosseri acknowledged it as 'a tricky problem' and stated directly: 'I am very worried about this trend.' The statement marks a significant inflection in Meta's AI content policy — shifting from active encouragement of AI-generated content toward acknowledging its systemic damage to the original creator ecosystem.

Mosseri identified a fundamental limitation in existing copyright claim tools when applied to AI mimicry. Many original creators — comedians, dancers, and stylistic artists of all kinds — have never formally registered copyright over their distinctive style, narrative voice, or performance approach, leaving them with no legal basis for protection. This makes AI mimicry difficult to address through traditional copyright channels. Rather than pursuing a blanket ban on AI content, the platform's stated response is to expand mandatory AI labeling and create incentive structures to encourage voluntary creator disclosure.

Notably, this policy tension reflects a genuine strategic contradiction within Meta itself. The company has committed approximately $600 billion to AI infrastructure investment and actively prompts users to generate AI images and videos within its platforms — making Meta itself a structural contributor to the slop problem it now publicly laments. If AI-generated content continues to dilute the value of original creator work, creators may ultimately migrate away from Meta's platforms, and the erosion of the creator ecosystem would meaningfully undermine Meta's advertising commercial value.

For Hong Kong agencies using AI tools to produce ad creatives, this development carries direct operational implications. As mandatory labeling expands, AI-generated ad assets face an increasing likelihood of being automatically flagged on Meta platforms, with labels visible to users — potentially affecting perceived credibility and brand perception. Additionally, AI ad creatives that closely mirror the visual style or narrative framing of known creators may face heightened platform scrutiny or takedown risk under evolving policies. Agencies should establish internal AI usage records within their creative workflow and develop a labeling disclosure strategy for each asset type, rather than waiting passively for policy enforcement to force a reactive response.

From a broader industry perspective, this episode signals that the trust perimeter for AI content in commercial contexts is narrowing. Advertiser and consumer tolerance for AI-generated content varies substantially by category — the impact of an AI label on a functional information ad versus one relying on brand emotional resonance will differ dramatically. Agencies should proactively align with clients on acceptable boundaries for AI creative use, building a documented decision framework that can withstand both stricter platform regulation and client scrutiny.

Meta Outlines Three Muse AI Business Use Cases: Ad Queries, Campaign Setup, and Shopify Automation

Since Yesterday

We covered Meta Muse Wonder's launch on Oct 13 and its enterprise pricing on Oct 14. Today Meta published three concrete business workflow examples — querying ad data, scheduling campaigns, and chaining Shopify and Canva — moving from product announcement to hands-on demonstration. Agencies now have concrete use-case templates to show clients.

Key Highlights
  • Muse enables natural language queries against ad account performance data, bypassing the Ads Manager interface.
  • Conversational campaign setup lets users configure ad placements via text commands, with current integrations covering Shopify, Canva, and HighLevel.
  • End-to-end automated funnels allow ad creation, publishing, and tracking within a single AI conversation interface, reducing tool-switching friction.
Industry Insight

Agencies can pitch clients on using Muse to link e-commerce and design tools, reducing manual ad ops.

Read our full write-up

Meta officially launched its Muse AI package for small and medium businesses in late September 2026, and has since published detailed breakdowns of three core commercial use cases. These are not conceptual blueprints — they represent high-frequency usage patterns already adopted by large numbers of SMB users, making them directly relevant competitive reference points for agencies.

The first and most frequently used scenario is ad performance assessment. Business owners can query Muse in natural language — asking 'Analyze my Meta ads. What are my highest performing ads and why?' or 'What are top recommendations to improve my Meta ads performance?' — and receive synthesized analysis without opening Ads Manager or manually parsing reports. The implications for agencies cut both ways: clients' ability to self-serve analysis may compress the perceived value of routine reporting interpretation services, while agencies themselves can use the same tool to accelerate internal analysis workflows.

The second scenario is conversational campaign building, where users upload a creative brief and receive dialogue-guided recommendations for campaign structure based on available assets and objectives. More strategically significant is the third scenario: cross-platform automated marketing funnels. Via native connectors to Shopify, Canva, and HighLevel, Muse can execute a complete sequence — analyzing product catalogs, determining creative direction, generating ad assets, and launching campaigns. Pixel tracking installation support is also included, meaning the initial configuration of an entire ad tech stack can be completed within the Muse environment.

Architecturally, Muse is designed to consolidate what were previously fragmented tools — Ads Manager, Business Manager, third-party e-commerce and design platforms — into a single natural language interface, reducing the cognitive overhead of context-switching across systems. For Hong Kong agencies, this signals a systematic erosion of the baseline service boundary for SMB clients, particularly in the areas of campaign setup, reporting interpretation, and basic optimization recommendations. At the same time, agencies that proactively master Muse's full capability can position it as a tool differentiator — demonstrating Shopify-integrated end-to-end automation from product listing to ad delivery is a workflow that can be immediately converted into a client proposal.

It is worth noting that Muse's current three use cases are primarily SMB-oriented, and its natural language query capability remains bounded by Meta's own advertising ecosystem data. It cannot integrate Google Analytics, third-party attribution tools, or client-side CRM data. Agencies retain a meaningful professional advantage in cross-channel perspective and complex attribution strategy that platform-native tools cannot yet replicate. The critical competitive positioning is to proactively frame Muse as an amplifier of agency service efficiency, rather than treating it as a threat to the agency role.

Amazon's TNF Two-Pronged Pitch: Streaming Reach for Non-Endemics, Closed-Loop Attribution for Brands

  • Non-Amazon brands access national sports audiences through TNF; endemic brands gain direct closed-loop attribution from ad exposure to on-site purchases.
  • Amazon TNF's competitive edge over traditional TV is direct purchase attribution from its retail data, not just reach scale.
Industry Insight

Amazon's closed-loop sports media model challenges traditional TV planning — evaluate Prime Video sports as a full-funnel buy.

Read our full write-up

Amazon's Thursday Night Football (TNF) has entered its fourth season, with the maturity of its advertising strategy now clearly visible. Advertiser count grew 6% year-over-year this season, with more than 80 brands new to TNF since 2022 — of which 54 were entirely new to Amazon's advertising ecosystem. This figure reveals that live sports on Prime Video functions as a significant new-client acquisition channel for Amazon's ad business, well beyond the logic of monetizing viewership alone. The Week 4 matchup (Pittsburgh Steelers vs. Cleveland Browns) set a Prime Video record for that time slot, up 19% year-over-year versus the comparable 2025 Week 4 game, confirming that live sports is strengthening its hold on streaming audiences.

Amazon's pitch to advertisers is a two-pronged proposition with distinct value narratives for non-endemic and endemic brands respectively. For non-Amazon-ecosystem brands like American Express, TNF serves as a gateway into Amazon's broader ad network and DSP — enabling brands to test platform capabilities in a high-profile sports context before scaling learnings into wider DSP strategies. This path is particularly attractive to mid-sized brands priced out of traditional broadcast sports, such as Colgate, which had not run a Super Bowl campaign in over seven years but found an acceptable entry cost for national-scale reach through TNF.

Closed-loop attribution is Amazon's most structurally differentiated capability in the sports advertising context. Colgate's 2025 TNF results illustrate this clearly: by retargeting users who had been exposed to TNF spots using Amazon's first-party data — and serving contextually relevant retail assets in shopping environments — the campaign drove purchase rates up 8x, doubled branded searches, and lifted brand awareness by 3 percentage points. Traditional broadcast television cannot provide this kind of verified connection between upper-funnel exposure and lower-funnel purchase behavior, and Amazon's first-party retail data creates a structural moat that streaming competitors without e-commerce infrastructure cannot easily replicate.

For Hong Kong agencies serving retail, CPG, and e-commerce clients, this development carries direct strategic implications for media planning. Prime Video sports inventory should no longer be categorized solely as a brand awareness vehicle — it represents a full-funnel media buy in which large-scale upper-funnel reach and lower-funnel attributable conversion close within the same platform ecosystem. This is fundamentally different from traditional TV buying logic, where exposure ends at broadcast and effectiveness must be estimated through third-party research. Media planners should reclassify Prime Video sports as a full-funnel option in proposals for CPG and e-commerce clients, rather than treating it as a supplementary brand awareness channel.

A critical caveat worth maintaining: Horizon Media's buying specialist noted that TNF carries significant waste for brands with limited geographic distribution or products involving high-consideration purchase decisions. The value of a national-scale media buy is highly dependent on the advertiser's geographic coverage capability and product conversion cycle. When referencing the Colgate case study, agencies should assess whether client distribution footprint and product category characteristics are compatible with the preconditions of the closed-loop attribution model, rather than directly transposing case-study results to clients in different categories.

Target's adoption of TikTok-viral brand Mellow Sleep provides concrete data to underpin the proposition — widely discussed but previously short on hard evidence — that social media momentum directly influences retail buying decisions. Goodway Group's partnership with programmatic execution platform Fluency stands out as one of the most practically replicable agentic AI case studies available to mid-size agencies. After evaluating more than 70 AI-first vendors, the US-based agency adopted a strategic posture of 'own less, orchestrate more' — embedding its cross-channel expertise into Fluency's infrastructure to handle repetitive execution at scale. The core recognition was that an agency's competitive advantage lies in strategic insight, not in procedural campaign setup and asset trafficking.

Goodway describes the methodology as an 'intentional automation' framework, with its headline result being a complete inversion of the strategy-to-execution time ratio: from 10/90 to 90/10. A four-person cross-functional team compressed the brief-to-live timeline to under 1.5 weeks — roughly 50% faster than equivalent work that would previously have required additional staff working nights and weekends. Critically, this speed gain was achieved not by cutting quality checkpoints but by eliminating redundant steps; human review remains embedded throughout the architecture.

The governance principle underpinning the framework is a rules-first, LLMs-for-exceptions model: deterministic business rules govern all repeatable decisions, while large language models are reserved only for undefined edge cases, with a mandatory escalation path that flags a human to intervene. This reflects a practical concern about cost at scale — token costs become a real financial variable in large deployments, making a calibrated balance between LLM reliance and traditional software logic a prerequisite for sustainable operations.

What makes the Goodway model directly replicable is its staged implementation guidance. Agencies should audit processes before automating, assemble cross-functional teams spanning media, data, and leadership, validate rules with human review before expanding scope, and deliberately define what not to build — as Wolk's principle holds: 'just as important as what you do is what you choose not to do.'
