  • A new Ultrafast tier delivers up to 300 output tokens per second, billed at six times the standard rate.
Industry Insight

Frontier-class agentic quality at one-fifth the price makes automation you priced out last quarter viable now — reprice your AI line items.

Read our full write-up

Start with the actual structure of the discount, because it determines how an agency should reprice. GPT-6.1 Sol lists at $2 per million input tokens and $10 output, against GPT-6 Astra's $10 and $50 — exactly one-fifth. More significant is cached input, halved again from GPT-6 Sol's $0.20 to $0.10. For workflows that re-read the same brand guidelines and the same product feed every day, cached input is the dominant line on the real bill, so halving it matters more than the headline rate.

On quality, OpenAI's published figures show the factual error rate at low reasoning effort falling from 11.4% (GPT-6 Sol) to 7.7%, and staying within 1.9% of flagship Astra across all reasoning settings. The agentic benchmarks are the more relevant ones: on AutomationBench, which spans 47 tools across sales, marketing, ops, support, finance and HR, GPT-6.1 Sol scores 2.2 points above Claude Opus 5.5 at medium reasoning effort for roughly one-third the cost, while DeepSWE v1.1 comes in 6.4 points above GPT-6 Sol's best result. For a media team these numbers translate into reliability on multi-step work: report pulls, cross-market copy QA, campaign monitoring.

The new Ultrafast tier deserves a separate judgment. It pushes output to 300 tokens per second and bills at six times the standard rate. Do the arithmetic and Ultrafast still undercuts last week's Astra list price — meaning latency-sensitive scenarios (live-stream copy, on-the-call revisions in a client meeting) have an affordable option for the first time, while batch work should never touch that tier. Route the two tiers separately inside your workflow rather than picking one globally.

One availability caveat: GPT-6.1 Sol is open to Plus, Pro, Business, Enterprise and Edu users through ChatGPT Work and Codex, and is explicitly not in regular Chat yet. Teams automating through the API can switch and test today; colleagues working in the web interface cannot see it. OpenAI also released no GPT-6.1 Astra, which indirectly confirms last week's reported Astra 6.1 cancellation. The top tier paused this quarter while the workhorse tier cut prices sharply — so procurement logic has to change: stop assuming the most expensive option is the most appropriate one.

To see why this matters, start with a technical detail that rarely makes it into a proposal. Even with Zero Data Retention signed, one category of content was still retained: requests that tripped the safety review mechanism. Vendors needed to keep those for human review, which left an awkward gap in the contract — 'we retain nothing' with one exception, and that exception happened to be the most sensitive material. Private Safety Processing closes it: flagged content is still kept, now encrypted into the customer's own bucket, for 30 days, with the customer holding the keys.

Three technical points matter to legal. Decryption happens only inside a hardware-attested runtime, so even vendor staff cannot obtain plaintext in an ordinary environment. The content is explicitly barred from training. And the storage location is customer-nominated across AWS S3, Azure Blob or Google Cloud Storage — for regulated Hong Kong industries that means residency can be written into the contract rather than accepted as a vendor regional promise.

Be careful how this enters a pitch. Pricing was not disclosed, so treat it as a procurement conversation rather than a solved cost. The defensible framing: the technical path for data residency now exists, and the next step is confirming three things with the client's information-security team — whether their existing cloud contract already covers the nominated storage backend, whether a 30-day retention window fits their internal policy, and who owns key rotation on that bucket. Handing the client those three items is more persuasive than claiming generic residency support.

Also separate the two stages. ZDR with Private Safety Processing is available now to eligible API customers, having been previewed in August. Private Inference — where the inference itself runs inside the customer's control boundary — is still in preview and ships this autumn. If a client's requirement is that inference must not leave their environment, that belongs on a roadmap, not in a this-quarter commitment.

OpenAI, Amazon, Google and Apple All Absent From Nvidia's 100-Company Agent Safety Consortium

Since Yesterday

On 29 Sep we reported Nvidia launching the Open Agent Safety Platform with Anthropic and others. What moved: the absentee list is now the story. OpenAI, Amazon, Google and Apple all declined to sign publicly, because the full stack depends on the proprietary, non-open-source Sentry layer that runs only on Nvidia BlueField-4 DPUs. OpenAI instead backs a rival information-sharing group, Defense Factory, while privately co-developing OpenShell with Nvidia. What looked like industry consensus yesterday is two camps today.

Key Highlights
  • Over 100 companies joined, including Anthropic, Hugging Face, Arm and Intel. Absent: OpenAI, Amazon, Google, Apple.
  • Nvidia Sentry runs on BlueField-4 DPUs, works only on Nvidia silicon, and is not open source.
  • The rival Defense Factory group that OpenAI backs is itself supported by Anthropic, AWS and Google — the camp lines are not clean.
Industry Insight

There is no single agent-safety standard, so a brand deploying agents across vendors inherits several incompatible governance regimes.

Read our full write-up

Yesterday this read as the industry finally agreeing on agent safety: over 100 companies, including Anthropic, Hugging Face, Arm and Intel. One fact added today changes the whole assessment — OpenAI, Amazon, Google and Apple all stayed out. Those four happen to cover the agent platforms most enterprises actually run.

The technical reason is now clear. 'Open' applies to only part of the platform: the full stack depends on the Nvidia Sentry layer, which runs on BlueField-4 DPUs, works only on Nvidia silicon, and is not open source. For a company building its own inference chips or buying heavily from other vendors, joining means tying a safety guarantee to one supplier's hardware roadmap. Hugging Face CEO Clem Delangue publicly argued OpenAI's earlier rogue agents would have been caught sooner had the platform been deployed — a remark that also explains why OpenAI is not in it.

The camp lines are not clean, which matters most to anyone giving procurement advice. The Defense Factory group OpenAI backs is itself supported by Anthropic, AWS and Google, while Anthropic also appears on Nvidia's consortium list, and OpenAI is separately co-developing OpenShell with Nvidia. These are not two mutually exclusive alliances but overlapping partnerships, so no one's membership can be read as compliance with a standard.

Narrow the client advice accordingly: treat agent-safety attestations as vendor-specific and non-portable rather than as an industry standard. Two sentences worth putting into procurement documents. First, require the vendor to state whether its safety guarantee still holds on the client's own hardware or another cloud — the Nvidia layer does not hold on non-Nvidia silicon. Second, require the vendor to specify the audit-record format for agent actions and whether those records can be exported into the client's own security monitoring. In a multi-vendor reality the only thing that can be unified is the log, not the guarantee.

Industry Insight

Channel and audience selection is the craft agencies bill for, and it does not return once the simplified mode is default. Ask about auditability, measurement and fee disclosure now.

Read our full write-up

Start with the structure, because it decides next year's staffing. What Amazon did at its unBoxed conference in San Francisco was not add a feature — it merged two previously separate buying entry points. The DSP and the sponsored-ads console now collapse into a single platform, rebranded Amazon Ads Agent. After the merge, media planning, budget allocation and cross-placement optimisation are all handed to the platform's AI, and the human role narrows to approving creative. Launched alongside it were Full-Funnel Campaigns and DVA+, the latter consolidating Sponsored Display, Sponsored TV and programmatic display, video and audio into one buying unit. All three names point the same way: the manually adjustable dials are being put away and the platform decides instead.

What is notable is that the trade-off is stated by Amazon itself rather than inferred by observers. Inside Full-Funnel Campaigns advertisers can approve creative, and at the same time cannot nominate individual channels and cannot nominate specific audiences, receiving only format-level visibility. For a Hong Kong agency running Amazon for cross-border commerce or travel-retail clients, that single sentence removes precisely the most billable part of the craft: placement mix, audience layering, and moving budget between stages of the funnel. Clients used to pay professional fees for those judgments. Now those judgments happen inside the platform, the deliverable narrows to creative plus a results report, and the hours on your scope of work will be questioned by client procurement.

The timing makes caution more necessary. This simplified mode arrives while the Federal Trade Commission and 22 state attorneys general are suing Amazon, alleging undisclosed surcharges in its ad auctions caused Sponsored Products advertisers to overpay. Amazon disputes the allegation and the case is unresolved, so treat it as an unadjudicated claim. Put the two together and the implication is direct: in an environment where fee transparency is being challenged in court, advertisers are being asked to give up more visibility into channels and supply sources. Practically, raise three questions with the client side and your Amazon contact this quarter and ask for written answers — whether the agent's allocation decisions produce an exportable audit trail, whether third-party measurement still plugs in, and whether the disclosure level on fee structure and supply sources changes with it.

The last paragraph is our operating judgment rather than reporting: do not wait for the simplified mode to become the default before preparing. The sensible move is to split existing accounts into two groups, keeping one on the manually controllable path as a control and moving the other into Full-Funnel Campaigns, testing in parallel with the same creative over the same flight window so you build the baseline yourself instead of accepting the platform's after-the-fact account. Run the comparison across at least one full purchase cycle, or seasonality will mask the real difference. In parallel, rewrite the billing logic early: as placement and audience levers narrow, billable scope has to shift toward creative production, product-data quality, incrementality measurement design, and audit review of the platform's decisions. This is a repricing exercise, not an interface refresh — and the earlier the new line items go into renewal paperwork, the less ground you concede next year.

Pattern Watch

The ad asset is shifting from visual material to machine-readable product knowledge, merging creative output with the product database and making asset management and content governance one discipline.

Read our full write-up

The point of this story is not an extra placement — it is that the nature of the deliverable has changed. Branded Conversations are units placed inside the chatbot, carrying a brand's own colours, fonts, imagery and product descriptions through a multi-turn product discovery conversation. The companion mechanism, Conversational Briefs, is a free-text input where advertisers supply product features and use cases, and that input shapes how Amazon's AI describes and recommends the product. In other words, the asset actually being submitted is no longer a banner or a 15-second cut but a body of structured product knowledge. This is the first mainstream retail-media format of that shape, so the asset list has to be rewritten: feature comparison tables, use-case copy, bundling and add-on logic, and standard answers to common questions all become deliverables.

Be precise about where control stops, because it determines what you can promise a client. Amazon writes the opening sponsored prompt itself and the brand cannot specify it. More consequentially, brands cannot select which customer reviews the AI highlights — Amazon's answer to that question in the report was a single word: 'No'. The accompanying review-request product targets items with fewer than 1,000 reviews, caps review requests at 1,000, and bills cost-per-click, meaning a click that produces no review still costs money. Incentives such as samples or discounts are prohibited in those requests. Put the pieces together and the real structure is this: the brand pays to widen the review pool while having no say over which voices in that pool the AI uses to persuade the next buyer. Say that plainly in a pitch so the client does not treat it as a controllable word-of-mouth lever.

The item that most needs an internal process change is the nature of the brief. Amazon says it will surface console-level feedback on false or improper brief inputs, which means the text entered into Conversational Briefs is no longer marketing copy — it is a statement that the platform will review and that directly shapes how the AI speaks on the brand's behalf. Our judgment is to handle it as a compliance document: who drafts it, who reviews it, and how versions are archived should all be written down. Efficacy claims, ingredient descriptions, comparative language and regional applicability should pass the client's legal or quality function before entering the system. For Hong Kong and Taiwan clients with health, beauty or consumer-electronics lines this step cannot be skipped, because the same sentence carries different compliance thresholds across markets and the AI will repeat it to consumers who have no idea that distinction exists.

One sourcing caveat: this report currently rests on a single source, so verify it independently — particularly the review-request cap, the billing method and the prohibition on incentives, all of which should be confirmed in writing with your Amazon contact before you sign up for a test. As a low-risk starting point, do two things. First, pick one product line with a thin review count and enough margin to absorb the cost of unproductive clicks, run a small test, and compute cost per click divided by reviews actually obtained as your true unit cost rather than looking at the click price alone. Second, build a product-knowledge master document covering features, use cases, who the product suits and who it does not — that document is reusable across conversational ads, generative engine optimisation and customer-service bots, which makes it an investment rather than a one-off asset, and a natural starting point for the data-governance conversation with the client.

The hands-on step for this week is to prototype internally rather than on a client account. Pick a low-risk scenario that collects no personal data, such as a purely informational store guide, an internal event schedule, or a rules page that only displays content. Generate it from one sentence, then document the whole run: what description you typed, how far the output sat from expectation, how many revision rounds it took to become presentable, and whether any checkpoint can be inserted between preview and publish. That log is worth more than any external review, because it tells you which steps were genuinely removed and which merely moved. Alongside it, keep an internal list of which scenarios may ship through a generative flow and which always route to the dev team.

It is worth stating plainly what a one-sentence-generated Mini Program is not ready for. Anything that collects personal data, handles member login, payments or transactions, manages prize draws and fulfilment, or serves as a long-lived public entry point with uptime expectations should not be self-published directly, because the governance risk is the mirror image of the convenience: self-serve publishing routes around the development and legal review that used to catch data-collection and approval problems. Keep publish rights on one designated account, require a named reviewer to check data fields, privacy wording and campaign rules before anything customer-facing goes live, and write into the contract or statement of work who owns maintenance and fixes for output produced through a generative flow. That way you capture the cost saving without inheriting an accountability gap.

ChatGPT ad click-through topped 1% in four markets, none of them the US

Since Yesterday

On 25 Sep we reported ChatGPT Ads launching across seven Southeast Asian markets, with annualised revenue passing $1B in under 200 days. What moved: the first market-level performance data has arrived. Graphite, using Similarweb panel data, finds all four markets above 1% CTR sit outside the US, with Japan at 1.53% and South Korea at 1.21% — roughly double the US figure of 0.73%. Last week we knew where it had opened; today we know where people click. Southeast Asia and Taiwan only opened in late September, so nearby inventory remains unpriced.

Key Highlights
  • All-time CTR: Brazil 1.91%, Mexico 1.55%, Japan 1.53%, South Korea 1.21%.
  • English-language markets: Canada 0.75%, UK 0.74%, US 0.73%, Australia 0.67%, New Zealand 0.60%.
  • Measurement windows run from 5 to 25 weeks per market, no impression counts are disclosed, and rates are pooled per market rather than per campaign.
Industry Insight

Japan and Korea beating the US by roughly 2x is the first hard signal AI-chat inventory converts better outside English markets. Run a small test, not a benchmark.

Read our full write-up

Search Engine Journal reports on Graphite's State of ChatGPT Ads study, which is built on Similarweb panel data. The four highest all-time click-through markets are Brazil at 1.91%, Mexico at 1.55%, Japan at 1.53% and South Korea at 1.21%, while English-language markets sit clearly lower: Canada 0.75%, the UK 0.74%, the US 0.73%, Australia 0.67% and New Zealand 0.60%. The report also breaks out US impressions by category for 30 March to 4 August, with B2B SaaS taking 19.9% across 1,210 advertisers and Retail and Consumer Brands 10.4% across 1,510 advertisers. Japan and South Korea outperforming the US by roughly two times is the first hard signal that AI-chat ad inventory converts better in non-English markets.

How you read a headline CTR above 1% decides whether you make a bad call. Start with what it measures: these are all-time figures pooled per market, not split by campaign or placement, so nothing here tells you how any single creative performed. Next the measurement conditions: windows vary from 5 to 25 weeks by market, and window length alone shifts an average, with shorter windows more likely to preserve the novelty effect of new inventory. Then disclosure: no impression counts are published, so there is no way to tell whether a percentage rests on a few thousand impressions or a few million. Finally incentives: Similarweb sells the tracking tool behind the data, which does not make the numbers wrong, only means they are not a neutral industry benchmark. This is also a single-source report as we have it, so verify the original before citing it.

Practically, treat it as a reason to schedule a test rather than a basis for setting a KPI. First, establish your own baseline: put the same product line's click-through rate, cost per click and post-click conversion rate from existing search and social placements into one comparison table, because without that table 1.91% means nothing. Second, set a hard-capped test budget and write down the single deciding metric in advance, ideally qualified leads or orders after landing rather than CTR itself, since new inventory usually runs hot early and settles later. Third, fix a minimum observation period and a stop-loss line, and agree with the client up front that this is exploratory spend outside annual performance commitments. Fourth, align creative and landing page language, currency and payment methods with the target market, or the clicks are wasted.

For a Hong Kong advertiser the important part is which four markets these are. Japan and South Korea appearing on the list means the inventory already draws meaningful engagement from East Asian consumers, so clients selling into Japan or Korea, travel retail and cross-border commerce are the obvious first candidates, whereas Brazil and Mexico post higher rates with usually lower relevance to a local roster. At the same time OpenAI's late-September rollout across Southeast Asia and Taiwan leaves Greater China-adjacent inventory unpriced, and the relative cost advantage of an early test lives inside that window. Hong Kong itself appears in none of these figures, so any extrapolation is a hypothesis at best. The realistic plan is a small parallel test in Japanese, Korean and Traditional Chinese creative to build a local baseline from your own data, instead of importing someone else's market average into a media plan.
