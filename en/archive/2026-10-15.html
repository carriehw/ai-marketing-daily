Forty-Plus Publishers Join Global Coalition Demanding AI Licensing Fees for Journalism

Key Highlights
  • NYT, AFP, The Economist and 40+ major publishers united to demand AI companies pay licensing fees for journalism.
  • The coalition's goal is to prevent AI platforms from profiting by training models or generating responses from news without permission.
  • As the licensing dispute widens, agencies using RAG-based or AI news monitoring tools should monitor potential compliance cost increases.
Industry Insight

Rising content licensing costs will pressure AI platform pricing long-term, affecting agency AI tool and media budgets.

Read our full write-up

The announcement of additional publishers joining the coalition in October 2026 builds on a coordinated international front that had been forming for months. The New York Times, AFP, and The Economist represent anchor members, with over forty additional publishers adding their names at a moment that aligns closely with active AI copyright legislation cycles in multiple jurisdictions. The coalition's core demand is that AI companies must obtain licenses and pay fees before using journalistic content to train models or surface it within generative AI products — a framework modeled on the royalty licensing structures long established in music and film.

The legal action precedent was set by the New York Times, which filed a copyright infringement suit against OpenAI and Microsoft in late 2023. European publisher associations have since pursued parallel remedies under the EU AI Act and the EU Copyright Directive, pressing AI model providers to disclose training data sources and offer opt-out mechanisms. The AI companies in the coalition's crosshairs include OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google — whose large language models sit at the center of the dispute — as well as search engines and social platforms that deploy AI summarization features built on the same underlying content.

A number of licensing deals have already been struck, demonstrating that commercial resolution is possible: the Associated Press signed an agreement with OpenAI in 2023, followed by Axel Springer-owned outlets and Le Monde among European publishers. These precedents have shifted the coalition's strategy from outright opposition toward collective licensing negotiation — using unified membership to raise each outlet's individual bargaining power and avoid being picked off one by one at below-market rates.

The structural tension underlying the dispute is the impact of AI-generated summaries on news publishers' traffic and subscription conversion funnels. When users obtain news digests within AI assistant interfaces without visiting original articles, both advertising revenue and paywall conversion rates face downward pressure. This commercial threat gives the licensing demand a survival-economics dimension beyond pure intellectual property principle.

AFP's participation carries particular systemic weight: as one of the world's largest wire services, its content forms foundational training material for numerous AI models. A legal or legislative ruling supporting its licensing claims would reshape the legal landscape for AI training data broadly. Media law scholars have noted that no industry consensus yet exists on how licensing fees should be priced, and the three-way challenge of protecting journalism's sustainability without impeding AI development remains an open question for regulators, publishers, and technology companies alike.

Adobe: 57% of Southeast Asian Organisations Stuck in AI's 'Frozen Middle', Only 6% Qualify as Leaders

Key Highlights
  • 57% of Southeast Asian organisations are piloting AI but unable to coordinate for scale deployment.
  • Only 6.3% qualify as AI Leaders; just 8% have comprehensive ROI measurement for AI investments.
  • ~36% of AI proposals fail at IT evaluation; only 12% have a designated AI owner with budget authority.
Industry Insight

Diagnoses why AI investments stall — structural gaps in ownership and measurement are the real barrier, not tool access.

Read our full write-up

The Adobe 2026 State of AI Readiness Report was conducted by market research firm Incisiv, surveying 461 senior executives across Southeast Asia, with financial services and healthcare and life sciences among the identified industries. The research segments companies by AI readiness level, and a critical structural finding is that financial services and healthcare firms face nearly double the security review rejection rates compared to other sectors — reflecting the compounding compliance burden that regulated industries encounter when attempting AI deployment.

The "frozen middle" label captures a specific paradox: AI investment is growing (73% of respondents increased budgets year-on-year) while deployment velocity remains near-static. The bottleneck is governance architecture, not technology access. 78% of companies rely on a ticketing model — routing every AI tool request through sequential IT evaluation (rejection rate ~36%) and security review (rejection rate ~18%). Business unit enthusiasm is systematically drained through administrative gatekeeping. 60% of companies report that deploying a single AI tool takes six months or longer; roughly one-third require over twelve months. 81% of respondents say AI skills gaps are worsening or unchanged, and only 8% have established comprehensive ROI measurement frameworks — meaning most companies cannot demonstrate returns, nor build the data case to justify further scaling.

The defining characteristic of the 6.3% classified as AI Leaders is not a different technology stack but a structural condition that the broader population lacks: a designated AI owner with actual budget authority. 89% of Leaders have this in place, versus 12% of all respondents. This single organizational difference enables Leaders to circumvent ticket-queue bottlenecks, apply consistent deployment standards across business lines, and replace scattered pilot islands with accountable enterprise-wide adoption.

Adobe's recommendations follow directly from the diagnostic. The path forward involves: appointing an AI owner with real budget power; replacing ticketing workflows with business-IT collaboration models; measuring output quality rather than usage volume; proactively closing skills gaps rather than waiting for hiring cycles; and establishing governance structures before scaling rather than retrofitting them afterward.

The broader regional picture points to a structural trap that researchers describe as perpetual pilot syndrome. 48% of companies report still working on their first use case — meaning large shares of AI budget are being spent on restarting rather than iterating. Without an owner who can mandate cross-functional standardization, each successful departmental pilot stays siloed. Every new initiative must re-litigate stakeholder buy-in from scratch, consuming management bandwidth that could otherwise deepen existing deployments. The gap between the 73% increasing investment and the ~60% who feel underinvested is not a paradox — it reflects money flowing into a system that redistributes effort without compounding returns.

Google DeepMind and Singapore Government Launch On-Device AI Scam Detection Model

Key Highlights
  • Google DeepMind and GovTech Singapore debuted an on-device AI scam detection model at SICW 2026.
  • A fine-tuned Gemma model in a two-stage architecture processes everything on-device — no data sent to Google or the government.
  • Scams account for 50%+ of Singapore's crime reports; manipulation-based scams represent ~80% of financial fraud losses since 2019.
Industry Insight

Privacy-preserving on-device AI is becoming the government standard — signals tightening compliance expectations for brands handling consumer data.

Read our full write-up

Google DeepMind's collaboration with GovTech Singapore was publicly unveiled at the Singapore International Cyber Week (SICW 2026). The initiative directly addresses Singapore's staggering annual fraud losses exceeding SGD 4 billion, targeting scam categories that include investment fraud, fake job postings, impersonation of government officials, and social engineering attacks that mimic local banks and institutions.

The model's two-stage pipeline is designed to balance speed with analytical depth. The first stage deploys a lightweight classifier that continuously screens incoming messages with minimal battery impact, flagging suspicious content for further review. The second stage then engages a fine-tuned Gemma model to analyze the full multi-turn conversation context, a critical capability since many scammers rely on gradual manipulation across multiple exchanges rather than obvious single-message cues.

All inference runs entirely on the user's device, meaning neither GovTech nor Google can access conversation content at any point. Model training took place inside an isolated Google Cloud Platform environment called the Sovereign Sandbox, built and controlled by GovTech. Only anonymized scam reports and synthetic dialogue datasets were used, with no personally identifiable information involved in the training process.

The feature requires explicit user consent before activation and will be rolled out in phases within participating applications. Deployment beyond Singapore has not been formally committed to, though DeepMind noted the architecture is replicable in other national contexts. No accuracy or precision metrics were disclosed in the announcement. The project stands as a notable example of how sovereign data governance frameworks can be combined with state-of-the-art language model technology to produce trustworthy consumer-facing AI safety tools.

Key Highlights

  • Available as desktop app, web, and SDK; model available on HuggingFace at apus-ailab/APUS-Reflex.
Industry Insight

Free open-source agent with persistent memory — embeddable in marketing automation pipelines for faster multi-step task execution.

Read our full write-up

APUS AI (Qilin Hesheng) open-sourced Reflex Agent under the MIT license on October 13, 2026. The core innovation is the proprietary Reflex decision model, which handles four functions — routing, skill selection, result review, and execution oversight — replacing the prompt-engineering or hardcoded logic pipelines that typically control agent behavior. Rather than operating as a question-answering system, Reflex Agent runs in a continuous "decision → execution → feedback → re-decision" loop, keeping it goal-oriented across multi-step tasks rather than limited to single-turn responses.

The Continuum memory engine is Reflex Agent's most technically distinctive component. Conventional agents handling long tasks rely on compressed summaries to preserve context, and that compression inherently loses information. Continuum instead enables lossless memory retrieval across separate working sessions, meaning the agent can accurately recall prior operation details when resuming a complex, multi-day task. The "unlimited context" claim refers specifically to this cross-session persistence, not to expanding the underlying model's single-inference input window — a distinction the developers explicitly clarify.

On performance, APUS AI's internal testing places Reflex Agent at up to 3× the response speed of Claude Code and Codex in comparable tasks. The likely source of that gap is a shorter decision path: the Reflex model handles routing and tool selection without triggering a full language model inference cycle on every step, reducing per-step computational overhead. The system targets personal assistant-style programming tasks as its primary use case and runs across desktop, web, and SDK deployment modes.

APUS AI has a long background in mobile intelligence; this open-source release marks the company's first time extending its core agent framework to the broader developer community. The repository is live at GitHub under APUS-AI-Lab/APUS-Reflex-Agent, with the underlying model available on HuggingFace. Compared to other open-source agent frameworks such as AutoGPT or CrewAI, Reflex Agent's distinguishing approach is substituting a trained decision model for a prompt chain — developers can integrate their own tool sets and memory backends, and the framework's practical ceiling will depend on the pace and depth of community contributions.

Agency Timesheets Are Dying: WFA Survey Shows Labour-Based Fees Collapsed from 54% to 17% in 15 Years

Key Highlights
  • Labour-based remuneration among multinationals fell from 54% in 2011 to just 17% today, according to WFA & Agency Mania Solutions.
  • 58% plan to increase performance-based fees; 36% plan to expand fixed/output-based arrangements.
  • In media planning, 41% already use a labour-plus-performance hybrid; 36% report agency relationship management has grown more difficult.
Industry Insight

Fee restructuring directly impacts agency revenue — both brands and agencies need to redefine deliverables and accountability before next contract renewal.

Read our full write-up

The World Federation of Advertisers' latest compensation survey captures the full scale of a structural shift that has been building for fifteen years. Labour-based billing — where agencies charge according to staff hours worked — has declined from 54% adoption among multinationals in 2011 to just 17% today, a fall of more than two thirds. This is not a cyclical dip but a directional change driven by digitalisation, AI-enabled production, and the growing expectation from clients that agency compensation should connect to measurable business outcomes rather than inputs.

The transition is most advanced in media disciplines. Media planning leads the shift, with 41% of arrangements now using a hybrid labour-plus-performance model, followed by media buying at 34% and paid social at 30%. Fixed or output-based models account for 36% of arrangements. Despite the momentum, performance fees remain a relatively small component in practice: between 64% and 80% of respondents report that performance-linked fees still represent less than 20% of total compensation. The gap between stated intent and actual implementation points to structural barriers — specifically, the difficulty of designing contracts that fairly allocate accountability when data access remains asymmetric.

Named practitioners in the article each highlight a different dimension of this challenge. Johnny Ng of WPP Media Hong Kong frames the goal as connecting fees directly to business outcomes — bank applications approved, hotel rooms booked, store visits driven, incremental retail sales generated. Chris Greenough of GrowthOps Asia cautions that contracts alone cannot solve upstream problems: if client briefs are under-specified or the working relationship lacks trust, even well-designed performance structures will underdeliver. Will Lee of That Marketing Guy challenges the assumption that output-based billing is inherently fairer, pointing to the data asymmetry problem: an agency cannot be held accountable for a number it is not permitted to see. Jeffrey Lim of 8traordinary argues that brands should pay for strategy and creativity as distinct value categories, not simply count deliverables.

Artificial intelligence accelerates the urgency of resolving these questions. By compressing the time required for execution-heavy tasks — copy generation, media analysis, reporting — AI decouples effort from value in ways that make hourly billing look increasingly disconnected from what clients actually receive. Agencies face pressure to reposition their value proposition around judgment, strategy, and creative direction rather than production throughput. For marketers, the practical implication is that compensation negotiations in the coming years will hinge on two capabilities: the ability to define outcomes precisely enough to be measurable, and the willingness to share the data that makes accountability possible on both sides.

Google Ads Launches Channel Prioritization — Control Budget Flow and Set Caps Per Channel

Key Highlights
  • New 'High priority' and 'Low priority' labels for channels including Search, Shopping, Performance Max, and Demand Gen in Google Ads.
  • Low-priority channels can have a maximum daily budget percentage cap, preventing test inventory from cannibalizing core campaigns — works at account level and with shared budgets.
  • From February 2027, Google Display Network on Search campaigns should be set to Low priority with 50% budget cap to preserve current behavior.
Industry Insight

Gives precise budget control across channels and lets teams safely test new inventory without cannibalizing core campaign performance.

Read our full write-up

Google Ads' new channel prioritization feature gives advertisers a direct mechanism for controlling how shared budgets flow across campaign types. At both the account and campaign levels, advertisers can assign High or Low priority labels to Search, Shopping, Performance Max, and Demand Gen campaigns. The system allocates budget to high-priority channels first — up to their Target CPA or Target ROAS goals — before directing remaining funds to lower-priority channels. This replaces the previous model, where campaigns sharing a budget competed against each other for allocation without a structured hierarchy.

The low-priority budget cap is the most operationally significant aspect of the feature. Advertisers set a maximum percentage of daily budget for any channel designated as low priority. Google's documentation describes compliance as "best-effort" rather than a hard guarantee, meaning the system works to respect the cap but may flex slightly under certain auction conditions. When a low-priority channel's budget allocation is exhausted during the day, the remaining available budget is generally redirected toward high-priority channels, preventing core campaigns from running out of funds due to competition with secondary placements. Advertisers should monitor actual spend distribution actively rather than treating the cap as an absolute ceiling.

The relationship between channel prioritization and Smart Bidding requires careful interpretation. Google has clarified that for campaigns running Target CPA or Target ROAS that are not budget-constrained, assigning a priority level will generally not affect performance — these campaigns are already optimised by the bidding algorithm to spend efficiently within their goals. Channel prioritization becomes the primary lever when budget is the binding constraint: it is a tool for advertisers who need to make an explicit decision about which channels receive funding when there is not enough to go around, rather than leaving that decision to the system's efficiency optimisation.

For Performance Max and Demand Gen campaigns, the feature applies in the same way, with Google-owned surfaces — Search, YouTube, Maps, Gmail, and Discover — grouped together as "Google channels" for priority management purposes, simplifying configuration across what would otherwise be multiple individual placement considerations. The rollout is global and described as ongoing over the coming months, with no specific account eligibility criteria disclosed, suggesting broad availability is the intended end state.

The February 2027 deadline for GDN spend allocation changes makes this feature time-sensitive for some advertisers. Existing GDN spend allocation setups will be removed at that date; Google recommends that advertisers who want to preserve their current budget behaviour set GDN to Low priority with a 50% allocation cap before then. For advertisers who have not yet built structured budget frameworks, this deadline functions as a forcing event — an external prompt to evaluate whether current channel allocation logic reflects deliberate strategic choices or simply inherited defaults.
